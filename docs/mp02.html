Welcome to U.S. Energy! Today’s topic is the Green Transportation Awards. All public transportation providers across the U.S. are eligible for awards in 2025, and we’ll be revealing the best and worst performers of the year.

Let’s begin today’s program.

People are increasingly prioritizing green transportation and opting for public transport. However, public transport itself often comes with considerable carbon emissions. Despite various energy sources being explored, the cost remains high: the five states with the most expensive electricity are Hawaii, California, Connecticut, Rhode Island, and Massachusetts. The five states with the highest carbon intensity for electricity are West Virginia, Kentucky, Wyoming, Missouri, and Hawaii. Hawaii is on both lists, boasting both expensive and “dirty” electricity, making it a “double champion.”

Code 
AGENCY_EMISSIONS <- EMISSIONS |>
    group_by(`NTD ID`, Agency, state) |>
    summarize(total_CO2_lbs   = sum(total_CO2_lbs),
              total_upt       = sum(total_upt),
              passenger_miles = sum(passenger_miles),
              .groups = "drop") |>
    mutate(CO2_per_UPT = total_CO2_lbs / total_upt,
           CO2_per_PM  = total_CO2_lbs / passenger_miles)
  • Totaling: Added together the emissions from all modes of transportation (buses, subways, ferries, etc.) for the same organization.
  • Standardization: Divided by the Annual Passenger Trips (UPT) and Passenger Miles to obtain “emissions per passenger trip” and “emissions per passenger mile.”
Code 
AGENCY_EMISSIONS <- AGENCY_EMISSIONS |>

To avoid pitting elephants against ants, we eliminated extremely small organizations with fewer than 100,000 passengers annually and categorized the remaining organizations into three groups based on passenger volume: small (less than 1 million passengers), medium (1 million to 20 million passengers), and large (more than 20 million passengers).

Best Award: Greenest Transportation Organization of the Year

Large Group Winner: Tri-County Metropolitan Transportation District of Oregon (TriMet)

TriMet emits only 0.40 pounds of CO2 per passenger and an even lower 0.10 pounds per passenger-mile.

Portland’s remarkably low carbon footprint is attributed to both its light rail network and Oregon’s hydroelectric power grid. Traveling by TriMet truly offers a “green light” experience.

Mid-sized Group Champion: Anaheim Transportation Network (California)

Emissions are only 0.20 lbs per passenger trip and 0.14 lbs per passenger mile. Even in California, where electricity prices are among the highest in the nation, Anaheim ensures every kilowatt-hour is used efficiently, proving that “expensive” doesn’t equate to “dirty.”

Small Group Champion: Chicago Water Taxi (Wendella, Illinois)

Emissions are 0.94 lbs per passenger trip and 0.50 lbs per passenger mile. Who says green travel is only for land? This water taxi company has “ridden the waves,” dominating the small group and making commuting on the Chicago River a truly “green” experience.

Conclusion

Public transportation is a great help for green travel, but being “green” isn’t automatically achieved by simply taking the bus: energy mix, the state’s power grid, and passenger volume all influence the final carbon bill.
